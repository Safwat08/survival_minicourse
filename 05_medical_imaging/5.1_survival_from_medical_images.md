# Survival from Medical Images

Everything so far has treated the patient as a row of numbers: a covariate vector
$x$ of age, stage, biomarker values. In medical image computing the input is a
scan instead, whether a 2D radiograph, a 3D volume, or a 4D time series. This
section is about what changes when the covariates *are* the image.

Almost nothing changes except the last layer and the loss.

## Diagnosis vs. Prognosis

Clinicians read images for two kinds of question, and the distinction decides
which modelling task you are in.

| | **Diagnosis** | **Prognosis** |
|---|---|---|
| Question | What is happening *now*? | What will happen, and *when*? |
| Example | "Is this nodule malignant?" | "How long until this tumour progresses?" |
| Label | Available at scan time (biopsy, radiologist read) | Requires **follow-up** of months to years |
| ML task | Classification / segmentation | Time-to-event (survival) |
| Outcome shape | A class $y \in \{0,1\}$ | A pair $(T, \delta)$: time and event indicator |

That last row is why this course exists. A diagnostic label is a fact about the
image. A prognostic label is a fact about the *future*, which means:

- Some patients have not had the event yet when the study closes; their labels
  are **censored**, not missing. See [`1.2`](../01_background/1.2_censored_data.md).
- You must state a **prediction origin**: the clock has to start somewhere, and
  it is normally the scan date. See [`1.3`](../01_background/1.3_what_is_an_event.md).
- More than one thing can happen first: progression, death, transplant. See
  [`1.4`](../01_background/1.4_multi_events_and_competing_risks.md).

## Which Modality, and What It Is Good For

Modality choice is not a free parameter; it is set by the clinical question, the
organ, and what the hospital already acquires as standard of care. A prognostic
model is only deployable if its input is a scan that patients actually get. 
Many imaging modalities are utilized for different diagnostic and prognostic tasks, 
with their own use-cases and constraints. A few of the most common modalities are described below:

| Modality | What it measures | Typical diagnostic use | Prognostic signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CT** | X-ray attenuation, reconstructed as a 3D volume | Lung nodules, staging, trauma, tumour burden | Tumour texture and shape; change in burden across serial scans |
| **MRI** | Tissue magnetic properties; multi-sequence (T1, T2, FLAIR, DWI, contrast-enhanced) | Brain, prostate, breast, musculoskeletal (best soft-tissue contrast) | Glioma subtype and infiltration; enhancement patterns |
| **X-ray (radiography)** | 2D projection attenuation | Chest, fracture, screening (cheap, fast, ubiquitous) | Cardiothoracic and parenchymal findings linked to mortality risk |
| **Ultrasound** | Reflected sound; real-time, no ionising radiation | Cardiac, obstetric, thyroid and breast nodules, bedside | Echocardiographic function (e.g. ejection fraction) → cardiac outcomes |

## Survival Is a Head

A deep imaging model is conventionally split in two:

```
image  →  [ backbone ]  →  z ∈ R^d  →  [ head ]  →  prediction
```

The **backbone** (ResNet, U-Net encoder, 3D CNN, ViT, or a foundation-model
encoder) turns the image into an embedding vector $z$. The **head** is a small
final layer that maps $z$ to whatever the task needs. Swapping the task means
swapping the head and the loss; the backbone is largely indifferent.

| Task | Head | Output | Loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary classification | `Linear(d, 1)` | logit | binary cross-entropy |
| $K$-class classification | `Linear(d, K)` | logits over classes | cross-entropy |
| Regression | `Linear(d, 1)` | scalar | MSE |
| **Survival (Cox)** | `Linear(d, 1)` | log-relative hazard $\varphi(x)$ | [Cox partial likelihood](../02_loss_functions/2.1_cox_ph_loss.md) |
| **Survival (discrete-time)** | `Linear(d, K)` | hazard in each of $K$ time bins | [NLL](../02_loss_functions/2.2_negative_log_likelihood_loss.md) |
| **Survival (competing risks)** | `Linear(d, K×C)` | joint grid over $C$ causes × $K$ bins | [DeepHit](../02_loss_functions/2.4_deephit_loss.md) |

The middle rows are the ones that matter. A **Cox head is architecturally a
regression head**: one output neuron. A **discrete-time survival head is
architecturally a $K$-class classifier**, with tensor shapes identical to
predicting one of $K$ labels. You could take a working classification model,
change nothing but the loss function, and have a survival model.

The practical recipe:

1. Take any imaging backbone that works for your modality.
2. Replace the classification head with a survival head from the table above.
3. Replace cross-entropy with a survival loss, the subject of [`02`](../02_loss_functions/2.1_cox_ph_loss.md).
4. Evaluate with survival metrics, not accuracy, the subject of [`03`](../03_metrics/3.1_c_index.md).

### Transfer learning

Because only the head and the loss change, the **pretraining** half of a standard
imaging pipeline needs no changes at all. Whatever you would do to get a good
backbone for classification, you do here; only the final stage becomes
survival-specific. The workflow has two stages, and only the second one *has* to
see your survival labels:

1. **Pretrain the backbone** on a large image collection to learn general-purpose
   features. The pretraining objective does not have to match the objective you
   fine-tune with, so any of these works:
   - **Labelled pretraining**: train on a *different* task that does have labels,
     such as ImageNet classification or a large diagnostic dataset, then keep the
     backbone and throw away that task's head. Note that 2D ImageNet weights do not
     drop straight into a 3D network; you either inflate them across the third axis
     or start from a checkpoint that was pretrained on volumes.
   - **Self-supervised pretraining**: train on raw scans with **no labels at all**,
     using an objective the images supply themselves (reconstructing masked patches,
     or matching two augmented views of the same volume). This suits medical imaging
     because unlabelled scans are plentiful while outcome-labelled ones are scarce.
   - **Survival pretraining**: nothing prevents the pretraining objective from
     being a survival loss itself, on a larger or public cohort that has follow-up.
     When such a checkpoint exists it is the strongest available starting point,
     because the features were already shaped by the risk-ranking task.
2. **Fine-tune with the survival head attached.** Discard the pretraining head,
   attach a survival head from the table above, and keep training on your own cohort
   with a survival loss. This is the stage that needs your follow-up times and event
   indicators.

In practice the checkpoint you start from is usually one of the first two kinds: a
general-purpose backbone that was never trained on anything survival-related.
That is fine, and is the normal case rather than a compromise. Generic
self-supervised features transfer to survival perfectly well, because what
fine-tuning has to learn is the mapping from those features to a risk score, not
the features themselves.

Pretraining matters *more* here than in classification, because survival cohorts are
small twice over: few patients, and fewer **events**. Censoring means your effective
sample size is the number of observed events rather than the number of subjects
([`1.2`](../01_background/1.2_censored_data.md)), so a 300-patient study might carry
only 40 deaths' worth of signal. A 3D backbone has millions of parameters; pretraining
is what makes it trainable on a cohort that size.

If the event count is very small, **freeze** the backbone and train only the head (a
*linear probe*): far fewer parameters, so far less to overfit. Fine-tune the whole
network once you have enough events to support it.

[`5.2`](5.2_3d_mri_survival.md) is stage 2 in full, starting from a self-supervised
3D MRI checkpoint and fine-tuning it with a survival head.

## Failure Modes

The head swap is real, but a survival head is easier to break than a
classification head, and the breakages are quiet: most of them raise your reported
score rather than lower it. They fall into two families. The first three are ways
the **survival objective** fails to train or be measured properly. The last four
are ways information **leaks**, either across the prediction origin or across the
train/test boundary.

| # | Failure mode | Why imaging makes it worse | Symptom |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Risk set truncated by the batch | 3D volumes force batches of 2 to 8 | Cox loss is unstable; results move with batch size |
| 2 | Classification metrics on censored labels | Habit carried over from diagnosis work | "Accuracy" is reported; censored patients scored as negatives |
| 3 | More parameters than events | 3D backbones are large, event counts are small | Train C-index near 1.0, test C-index near 0.5 |
| 4 | Per-slice or per-scan split | One volume yields hundreds of near-duplicate slices | Excellent held-out C-index that vanishes on new patients |
| 5 | Outcome-aware annotation | ROIs are usually drawn retrospectively | Model beats clinical baselines by an implausible margin |
| 6 | Preprocessing fit on all the data | Harmonisation and feature selection are dataset-level by default | Optimistic cross-validation, poor external validation |
| 7 | Information from after the origin | Follow-up scans sit in the same archive as baseline ones | Very high performance driven by one post-origin variable |

### Objective and metric failures

**1. The Cox loss is not per-sample.** Cross-entropy decomposes over examples:
each image's loss is computed independently, so batch size affects only gradient
noise. The Cox partial likelihood does not decompose. It compares each patient
against the **risk set** of everyone still event-free at that time, so the loss
couples samples *within a batch*. 3D volumes are large, which forces small
batches, which means each batch contains a poor approximation of the true risk
set. This is frequently underestimated; mitigations are discussed in
[`2.1`](../02_loss_functions/2.1_cox_ph_loss.md). Discrete-time losses
([`2.2`](../02_loss_functions/2.2_negative_log_likelihood_loss.md)) do decompose
per-sample, which is a reason to prefer them for imaging.

**2. Censored labels are not negative labels.** Accuracy is undefined here; a
patient censored at 6 months has not been observed to be event-free forever, so
scoring them as a negative class is simply wrong, and it biases the score in an
uncontrolled direction. Binarising the outcome at a fixed horizon and reporting
plain ROC-AUC has the same defect. Use the metrics in
[`03`](../03_metrics/3.1_c_index.md), which are built to consume $(T, \delta)$:
C-index for discrimination, time-dependent AUC at a stated horizon, and Brier
score for calibration.

**3. Parameters vastly outnumber events.** Censoring makes the effective sample
size the number of observed **events**, not the number of patients, so a 3D
backbone with millions of parameters is often being fit to a few dozen
informative labels. This is the single most common reason an imaging survival
model does not replicate. The mitigations are the ones in
[transfer learning](#transfer-learning) above: pretrain the backbone, and freeze
it as a linear probe until the event count justifies fine-tuning the whole
network.

### Leakage failures

The prediction origin is usually the scan date, so everything the model sees must
have existed **at or before** that date, and nothing that identifies a patient may
appear on both sides of a split.

**4. Splitting.** Split by *patient*, never by slice or by scan; the same
patient's slices must never straddle train and test. Split by *site* as well when
you intend to claim geographic generalisation, since scanner and protocol
differences are a shortcut the model will happily take if site correlates with
outcome.

> **The most common silent failure in imaging survival work is a per-slice split.**
> A 3D volume contributes hundreds of correlated slices. Splitting on slices puts
> near-duplicate images in both train and test, and the C-index looks excellent
> until the model meets a new patient.

**5. Annotation.** If an ROI was drawn by someone who knew the outcome, that
knowledge can leak through the mask, and the model reads the annotator's
hindsight rather than the image.

**6. Preprocessing.** Fit intensity normalisation, feature selection, and
harmonisation on the training split only. Per-case operations (resampling,
cropping, per-image normalisation) are safe; anything estimated *across* cases is
not.

**7. Acquisition.** No scan taken after the origin, and no covariate recorded
after it either. Baseline and follow-up scans are usually stored side by side, so
this one is easy to do by accident.
