Survival from Medical Images

Everything so far has treated the patient as a row of numbers: a covariate vector x of age, stage, biomarker values. In medical image computing the input is a scan instead, whether a 2D radiograph, a 3D volume, or a 4D time series. This section is about what changes when the covariates are the image.

Almost nothing changes except the last layer and the loss.

Diagnosis vs. Prognosis

Clinicians read images for two kinds of question, and the distinction decides which modelling task you are in.

Diagnosis Prognosis
Question What is happening now? What will happen, and when?
Example “Is this nodule malignant?” “How long until this tumour progresses?”
Label Available at scan time (biopsy, radiologist read) Requires follow-up of months to years
ML task Classification / segmentation Time-to-event (survival)
Outcome shape A class y \in \{0,1\} A pair (T, \delta): time and event indicator

That last row is why this course exists. A diagnostic label is a fact about the image. A prognostic label is a fact about the future, which means:

  • Some patients have not had the event yet when the study closes; their labels are censored, not missing. See 1.2 Censored Data.
  • You must state a prediction origin: the clock has to start somewhere, and it is normally the scan date. See 1.3 What is an Event?.
  • More than one thing can happen first: progression, death, transplant. See 1.4 Multi-Events and Competing Risks.

Which Modality, and What It Is Good For

Modality choice is not a free parameter; it is set by the clinical question, the organ, and what the hospital already acquires as standard of care. A prognostic model is only deployable if its input is a scan that patients actually get. Many imaging modalities are utilized for different diagnostic and prognostic tasks, with their own use-cases and constraints. A few of the most common modalities are described below:

Modality What it measures Typical diagnostic use Prognostic signal
CT X-ray attenuation, reconstructed as a 3D volume Lung nodules, staging, trauma, tumour burden Tumour texture and shape; change in burden across serial scans
MRI Tissue magnetic properties; multi-sequence (T1, T2, FLAIR, DWI, contrast-enhanced) Brain, prostate, breast, musculoskeletal (best soft-tissue contrast) Glioma subtype and infiltration; enhancement patterns
X-ray (radiography) 2D projection attenuation Chest, fracture, screening (cheap, fast, ubiquitous) Cardiothoracic and parenchymal findings linked to mortality risk
Ultrasound Reflected sound; real-time, no ionising radiation Cardiac, obstetric, thyroid and breast nodules, bedside Echocardiographic function (e.g. ejection fraction) → cardiac outcomes

Survival Is a Head

A deep imaging model is conventionally split in two:

image  →  [ backbone ]  →  z ∈ R^d  →  [ head ]  →  prediction

The backbone (ResNet, U-Net encoder, 3D CNN, ViT, or a foundation-model encoder) turns the image into an embedding vector z. The head is a small final layer that maps z to whatever the task needs. Swapping the task means swapping the head and the loss; the backbone is largely indifferent.

Task Head Output Loss
Binary classification Linear(d, 1) logit binary cross-entropy
K-class classification Linear(d, K) logits over classes cross-entropy
Regression Linear(d, 1) scalar MSE
Survival (Cox) Linear(d, 1) log-relative hazard \varphi(x) Cox partial likelihood
Survival (discrete-time) Linear(d, K) hazard in each of K time bins NLL
Survival (competing risks) Linear(d, K×C) joint grid over C causes × K bins DeepHit

The middle rows are the ones that matter. A Cox head is architecturally a regression head: one output neuron. A discrete-time survival head is architecturally a K-class classifier, with tensor shapes identical to predicting one of K labels. You could take a working classification model, change nothing but the loss function, and have a survival model.

The practical recipe:

  1. Take any imaging backbone that works for your modality.
  2. Replace the classification head with a survival head from the table above.
  3. Replace cross-entropy with a survival loss, the subject of 02 Loss Functions.
  4. Evaluate with survival metrics, not accuracy, the subject of 03 Metrics.

Transfer learning

Because only the head and the loss change, the pretraining half of a standard imaging pipeline needs no changes. Only the second of the two stages has to see your survival labels.

1. Pretrain the backbone. The pretraining objective does not have to match the one you fine-tune with, so any of these works:

  • Labelled — a different task that has labels (ImageNet, a large diagnostic cohort); keep the backbone, discard its head. 2D ImageNet weights need inflating across the third axis before they fit a 3D network, or start from a volume checkpoint.
  • Self-supervised — no labels, using an objective the images supply themselves (masked-patch reconstruction, matching augmented views). Suits medical imaging: unlabelled scans are plentiful, outcome-labelled ones scarce.
  • Survival — a survival loss on a larger cohort with follow-up. The strongest start, though such checkpoints are rare.

2. Fine-tune with the survival head attached. Discard the pretraining head, attach a head from the table above, and train with a survival loss. This is the stage that needs (T, \delta).

Pretraining matters more here than in classification: censoring makes your effective sample size the event count, not the subject count (see 1.2 Censored Data), so a 300-patient study might carry only 40 deaths’ worth of signal against a backbone with millions of parameters. If events are very few, freeze the backbone and train only the head (a linear probe); fine-tune fully once the event count supports it.

Failure Modes

A survival head is easier to break than a classification head, and the breakages are quiet: most of them raise your reported score rather than lower it. Modes 1–3 are ways the survival objective fails to train or be measured; modes 4–7 are ways information leaks, across the prediction origin or across the train/test boundary.

# Failure mode Why imaging makes it worse Symptom
1 Risk set truncated by the batch 3D volumes force batches of 2 to 8 Cox loss is unstable; results move with batch size
2 Classification metrics on censored labels Habit carried over from diagnosis work “Accuracy” is reported; censored patients scored as negatives
3 More parameters than events 3D backbones are large, event counts are small Train C-index near 1.0, test C-index near 0.5
4 Per-slice or per-scan split One volume yields hundreds of near-duplicate slices Excellent held-out C-index that vanishes on new patients
5 Outcome-aware annotation ROIs are usually drawn retrospectively Model beats clinical baselines by an implausible margin
6 Preprocessing fit on all the data Harmonisation and feature selection are dataset-level by default Optimistic cross-validation, poor external validation
7 Information from after the origin Follow-up scans sit in the same archive as baseline ones Very high performance driven by one post-origin variable

Two of these need more than a table row.

Why mode 1 is specific to Cox. Cross-entropy decomposes over examples, so batch size only affects gradient noise. The Cox partial likelihood does not: it compares each patient against the risk set of everyone still event-free at that time, coupling samples within a batch, so small batches approximate the risk set poorly. Discrete-time losses (2.2) do decompose per-sample, a reason to prefer them for imaging; Cox mitigations are in 2.1.

The leakage rule behind modes 4–7. Everything the model sees must have existed at or before the prediction origin, usually the scan date, and nothing identifying a patient may appear on both sides of a split. Per-case preprocessing (resampling, cropping, per-image normalisation) is safe; anything estimated across cases (harmonisation, feature selection, intensity statistics) must be fit on the training split only.

Mode 4 is the most common silent failure in imaging survival work. A 3D volume contributes hundreds of correlated slices. Splitting on slices puts near-duplicate images in both train and test, and the C-index looks excellent until the model meets a new patient. Split by patient, and by site too if you claim geographic generalisation.
