# Brier Score

The Brier score measures **overall probabilistic prediction error** at a time
horizon. It is affected by both calibration and discrimination: well-calibrated
probabilities can still score poorly if they do not distinguish patients, and a
well-ranked model can score poorly if its probabilities are miscalibrated. Use a
calibration curve when calibration itself is the target.

## Definition

At a fixed time $t$, the Brier score is the **mean squared error** between the predicted survival probability and the observed outcome:

$$
BS(t) \;=\; \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(
\underbrace{S(t \mid x_i)}_{\text{predicted prob. of surviving past } t}
\;-\;
\underbrace{\mathbf{1}(T_i > t)}_{\text{1 if actually survived past } t,\ \text{else } 0}
\Big)^2
$$

The indicator $\mathbf{1}(T_i > t)$ is the ground truth: 1 if subject $i$ was still event-free at $t$, 0 if the event had already happened. A perfect model predicts $S(t \mid x_i) = 1$ for survivors and $0$ for those who failed, giving $BS(t) = 0$.

> **Worked example: one time point.** Evaluate at $t = 12$ months for three patients who all survived past 12 (so each ground-truth indicator is 1):
>
> | Patient | Predicted $S(12)$ | Truth $\mathbf{1}(T>12)$ | Squared error |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | A | 0.9 | 1 | $0.01$ |
> | B | 0.6 | 1 | $0.16$ |
> | C | 0.8 | 1 | $0.04$ |
>
> $BS(12) = (0.01 + 0.16 + 0.04)/3 \approx 0.07$. Patient B hurts the score most; the model under-predicted a survivor's chances.

## Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW)

The raw score above breaks for **censored** subjects: if subject $i$ is censored
before $t$, their status at $t$ is unknown. Let $\hat G(u)$ estimate the probability
of remaining uncensored through $u$. The usual IPCW estimator is:

$$
BS_{\text{IPCW}}(t) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\left[
\frac{\mathbf{1}(T_i \le t,\,\delta_i=1)}{\hat G(T_i^-)}\,\hat S(t\mid x_i)^2
+
\frac{\mathbf{1}(T_i > t)}{\hat G(t)}\,\big(1-\hat S(t\mid x_i)\big)^2
\right]
$$

That is a lot of notation for one idea, and you do not need to be able to
reproduce it from memory. The idea:

> **IPCW lets each subject you *can* score stand in for the similar subjects you lost.**

Dropping censored subjects and averaging over whoever is left is not a neutral
choice: the remaining cohort is skewed toward people who stayed in the study
longest. IPCW corrects for that by counting each scorable subject more than once,
in proportion to how much of the cohort has gone missing by their time.

Everything else in the equation is bookkeeping. The two bracketed terms are the
only two ways to know a subject's status at $t$: their event already happened
(left term, truth $= 0$, so the error is $\hat S$), or they are known event-free
(right term, truth $= 1$, so the error is $1 - \hat S$). The indicators just
select which one applies. Subjects censored before $t$ match neither indicator,
so they drop out of the sum and are accounted for by the upweighting instead.

> **Worked example: reading a weight.** Say $\hat G(12) = 0.8$, meaning 20% of the
> cohort has been lost to follow-up by month 12. A patient known to be event-free at
> 12 gets weight $1/0.8 = 1.25$: they count as themselves plus a quarter of a
> censored peer. If follow-up were worse and $\hat G(12) = 0.5$, that same patient
> would count *twice*. This is also why late evaluation times are dangerous: as
> $\hat G$ approaches 0, a handful of survivors carry enormous weight and the score
> becomes unstable (see the pitfall below).

The correction is only as good as $\hat G$, which the packages estimate from
training follow-up under the same independent-censoring assumption as everything
else here (see [1.2 Censored Data](../01_background/1.2_censored_data.md#the-assumption-behind-standard-methods)).

## Integrated Brier Score (IBS)

A single time point only tells part of the story. The IBS averages $BS(t)$ across a time range $[t_{\min}, t_{\max}]$:

$$
IBS \;=\; \frac{1}{t_{\max} - t_{\min}} \int_{t_{\min}}^{t_{\max}} BS(t)\, dt
$$

This is a standard one-number summary of probabilistic prediction error across a
specified follow-up window. Always report that window because IBS values from
different intervals are not directly comparable.

The dashed line is the error you get from predicting the same cohort-average survival curve for everyone; the gap below it is what your covariates are worth. The mean height of the shaded band, not its raw area, is the IBS. The 0.25 line marks predicting 0.5 for everyone, which is a landmark, not a pass mark.](../assets/figures/3.2_brier_curve.svg){fig-alt="Two error curves rising over 36 months: a lower solid model curve with a shaded band beneath it, a higher dashed curve for the Kaplan-Meier prediction, a double-headed arrow marking the gap between them, and a dotted 0.25 reference line."}

**How to read it.** The most common misreading is to see "Kaplan–Meier" and expect
a survival curve falling from 1 toward 0. There is no survival curve on this plot.
Both lines are *prediction error*, and both rise over time because predicting far
into the future is harder than predicting the near term.

The dashed line answers "how wrong would I be with no model at all?" Take the
[KM curve](3.3_km_curves.md) for the whole cohort, hand every patient that same
$\hat S(t)$ regardless of their covariates, and score it. That is the honest
floor: any model that costs you a data pipeline and a training run should sit
below it.

So there are two things to read off the figure, and only the second is a number
you report:

- **The vertical gap** between the curves is the value your covariates add. A model
  curve that converges onto the dashed line at long horizons is telling you the
  covariates stop being informative out there, which no single summary number
  would reveal.
- **The mean height of the shaded band** is the IBS, matching the $1/(t_{\max}-t_{\min})$
  factor in the equation above. It is a time-average, not an area, which is why it
  stays on the same 0-to-1 scale as $BS(t)$ itself and why the window has to be
  reported alongside it.

## Interpretation

- **Lower is better:** $0$ is perfect. Predicting 0.5 for everyone produces 0.25,
  but an uninformative prevalence-based prediction can score below 0.25 when the
  outcome is imbalanced; a poor model can score above 0.25.
- Compare against a **Kaplan–Meier baseline** fit without covariates (see [3.3 Kaplan-Meier Curves](3.3_km_curves.md)) on the same evaluation window.

## Key takeaways for applied ML scientists

- **It is not a calibration metric by itself.** Report it alongside the [C-index](3.1_c_index.md) and a calibration curve or calibration slope/intercept at clinically relevant horizons.
- **Use the IPCW version with any real (censored) data**, since the raw score is biased. The packages do this for you, but they need the *training* survival arrays to estimate the censoring weights.
- **Keep evaluation times inside the follow-up window** (see pitfall below). This is the single most common way to get a meaningless Brier score.
- **Prefer the IBS for model selection** when performance across a window matters; use individual $BS(t)$ values to diagnose when prediction error grows.

## Implementation

**scikit-survival** is the reference implementation. Both functions take the *training* and *test* structured arrays (for the IPCW censoring estimate) plus the model's predicted survival probabilities `S(t)` at each evaluation time (see [5.4 scikit-survival](../05_packages/5.4_scikit_survival.md)):

```python
from sksurv.metrics import brier_score, integrated_brier_score
import numpy as np

times = np.percentile(time_test[event_test], [25, 50, 75])
# Also verify that these times lie inside the test follow-up range and the
# support of the censoring distribution estimated from y_train.
surv_prob = np.vstack([fn(times) for fn in model.predict_survival_function(X_test)])

t, bs = brier_score(y_train, y_test, surv_prob, times)          # BS at each time
ibs   = integrated_brier_score(y_train, y_test, surv_prob, times)  # single summary
```

**torchsurv** provides a native-PyTorch/GPU implementation (see [5.3 torchsurv](../05_packages/5.3_torchsurv.md)); compute it on
the accumulated evaluation cohort rather than averaging separate batch scores.
**auton-survival** includes IBS and reporting utilities (see [5.5 auton-survival](../05_packages/5.5_auton_survival.md)).

> **Pitfall: evaluation times.** Keep `times` within test follow-up and within the
> range over which the training censoring distribution is estimable. Near the tail,
> few subjects remain at risk and IPCW weights can become unstable; choose a
> clinically relevant, pre-specified window with adequate support rather than using
> the maximum follow-up mechanically.

> **Brier complements, not replaces, the C-index.** A model can rank well yet have
> high probability error. Report both, and add a calibration plot when calibration
> is a stated goal. See [3.1 Concordance Index (C-index)](3.1_c_index.md).
